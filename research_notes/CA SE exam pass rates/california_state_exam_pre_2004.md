# California state-written Structural Engineer (S.E.) exam pass rates, 1930s-2003

Researcher's summary up front: **no administration-by-administration table of candidates / passers / pass rate for the California-written SE exam was retrieved.** Every primary source likely to hold one (California Regulatory Law Reporter issues, Wayback snapshots of the old Board statistics pages, sunset reports from 1996-2003) either refused automated fetching or is not online. What was obtained is a small set of qualitative statements, one of which (Degenkolb) is directly relevant to the February 1985 low. The blocked sources are listed at the end so they can be fetched by hand.

Evidence labels used below: **[PRIMARY]** = stated in a Board or legislative document; **[RECOLLECTION]** = a practitioner's memory or estimate in an interview; **[SECONDARY]** = a later article with no source given; **[SNIPPET]** = seen only in a search-engine summary because the page itself returned 403, so wording and context are unverified.

## What were the candidate counts and pass rates for the California SE exam in each year where a figure survives (1930s-2003)?

### Takeaway
No year-by-year figure (candidates, passers, pass rate, exam date) was found for any single administration between 1931 and 2003. The only numbers recovered are practitioner recollections: "30+%" passing under curve grading falling to "10%" at the first non-curve exam (Degenkolb, mid-1980s), and unverified forum recollections of 7-20%.

### Cited Findings
- **[RECOLLECTION] Mid-1980s, pass rate before and after the end of curve grading.** Henry Degenkolb, interviewed by Stanley Scott between 30 January 1984 and 21 May 1986, said the Board of Registration was changing from grading examinations on a curve (a set share of each group passes) to a fixed minimum standard, and that with the first test done the new way "the number passing dropped from 30+% to 10%." He introduces it with "I think", so it is his impression, not a quoted statistic. He does not name the exam; the surrounding passage is about the Board's licensing exams in general, in a chapter that discusses the structural exam. Published volume p. 161 (Chapter 11, "Licensing and Continuing Education"). — [Connections: The EERI Oral History Series, Henry J. Degenkolb (EERI, 1994)](https://www.eeri.org/connections-eeri-oral-history-series); local copy already in the project as `research/sources/EERI_Oral_History_Degenkolb.pdf` (no direct PDF URL verified in this pass)
- **[RECOLLECTION] Firm-level first-time pass rate, not a statewide rate.** Roy G. Johnston (Brandow & Johnston, Los Angeles; Board of Registration member 1970-78) said that when his firm's younger staff take the structural exam, about 90 percent pass the first time. This describes one firm's staff, in the context of the firm's training practice, with no year given (interviews by Stanley Scott, published 2012). It must not be read as a statewide pass rate. Published volume p. 21. — [EERI Oral History, Roy G. Johnston (2012)](https://www.eeri.org/images/oralhistory/20_Johnston.pdf)
- **[SECONDARY, unsourced] "Twenty five percent" first-time pass rate at the time Ruth Gordon Schnapp passed.** A 2023 biographical article states that only 25 percent of those who took the exam passed on the first attempt, and that she was one of them. The article gives neither the exam year nor a source for the figure (its further reading lists Anna Lewis, *Women of Steel and Stone*, 2014, and a 2006 Society of Women Engineers oral interview). — [WIS Archive, "Engineering at the Epicenter: Ruth Gordon Schnapp"](https://www.wisarchive.com/post/engineering-at-the-epicenter-ruth-gordon-schnapp-california-s-first-woman-structural-engineer)
- **[SNIPPET, RECOLLECTION] Forum recollection of about 16%, and one year of about 7%.** A search summary of an Eng-Tips post reports a commenter recalling a pass rate of about 16% when they took the California-specific SE exam, and one year at about 7%. Page returned 403; poster, date and exam year not verified. — [Eng-Tips post](https://www.eng-tips.com/goto/post?id=9056373)
- **[SNIPPET, RECOLLECTION] Forum recollection of "between 7 - 20% passing".** A search summary of another Eng-Tips thread quotes a poster saying the pass rate at that time was 7-20%. The thread is about the civil seismic/surveying exams, the era is vague, and the page returned 403, so it is unclear whether this refers to the SE exam at all. — [Eng-Tips thread 277155](https://www.eng-tips.com/threads/ca-seismic-principles-amp-engineering-surveying-exam.277155/)
- **[RECOLLECTION] Individual exam dates only (no rates).** John F. Meehan says he passed the structural exam in 1951 (seen in search summary of the EERI volume, not opened). — [EERI Oral History, John F. Meehan](https://www.eeri.org/images/oralhistory/EERI-John-Meehan-Aug-2017-ONLINE.pdf). John Rinne says he graded papers for the civil exam at Henry Dewell's request, passed the civil exam (structural option) "readily", and "applied for and received" a structural license about two years later; he gives no pass rate or candidate count (p. 98). — [EERI Oral History, Michael V. Pregnoff and John E. Rinne](https://eeri.org/images/oralhistory/pregnoff.pdf)
- **[RECOLLECTION] Scale of candidate pool.** Degenkolb says that when California was a small state the profession relied heavily on references as well as examinations, and that by the mid-1980s "literally hundreds" were taking the exams (p. 159). No count is given. — [EERI Oral History, Degenkolb (1994)](https://www.eeri.org/connections-eeri-oral-history-series)
- **Context only, not SE:** the old Board statistics page (`pels.ca.gov/applicants/multistats.shtml`) did publish first-time vs. repeat-taker splits; a 2011 forum post quotes its 2002 figures for the FE/EIT exam (1,766 first-timers, 66.48% pass; 1,390 repeaters, 25.76% pass). This shows the kind of table that existed on the Board site and may have covered the SE exam too. — [engineerboards.com thread 17245, post by "mudpuppy", 19 Oct 2011](https://engineerboards.com/threads/what-exactly-are-the-pass-rates.17245/)

### Inferences
- The Degenkolb "30+% to 10%" statement is the only recovered number that lines up with the dashboard's February 1985 batch of 26 licenses. The interview window (1984-1986) and his phrase that the Board had "just changed over, or are in the process of changing" fit a fall-1984 exam whose passers were licensed in February 1985. This is an inference: he names neither the exam nor the date.
- The practitioner figures cluster in two bands: roughly 25-30+% (Schnapp-era secondary claim; Degenkolb's pre-change figure) and roughly 7-20% (Degenkolb's post-change 10%; forum recollections). None can be assigned to a specific year.
- Rinne's wording suggests that in the mid-1930s some structural licenses were obtained by application on experience, not only by sitting an exam; he does not say so explicitly.

### Gaps
- No candidate count, passer count, pass rate or exam date for any individual administration, 1931-2003. Record every year as a gap.
- Whether Degenkolb's "30+% to 10%" refers to the SE exam, the civil exam, or Board exams generally.
- The year Ruth Gordon Schnapp passed and the origin of the 25% figure.
- The Board's own 2002 (and earlier) SE statistics on the retired `multistats.shtml` page: not retrievable in this pass (see blocked sources).

## How did scoring change over time (curve grading, cut scores, the 1985 end of curve grading, criterion-referenced scoring), and what happened to pass rates around 1984-1986?

### Takeaway
One practitioner source (Degenkolb, speaking 1984-86) says the Board was abandoning bell-curve grading in favor of a fixed minimum standard and that the first exam under the new method cut the share passing from about 30+% to about 10%. No Board document describing the change, its date or its cut-score method was retrieved.

### Cited Findings
- **[RECOLLECTION]** Curve grading meant "a certain percentage of each group tested will pass, based on the bell curve"; the replacement was that everyone must meet "certain minimum requirements to qualify". First test under the new method: share passing fell from 30+% to 10% ("I think"). Degenkolb describes the Board as "in the throes of a lot of trouble" over it. p. 161. — [EERI Oral History, Degenkolb (1994)](https://www.eeri.org/connections-eeri-oral-history-series)
- **[RECOLLECTION] Format in the 1940s.** Degenkolb: when he took it the structural exam was a 16-hour test, following a separate 16-hour civil test, and both were open book; he calls them demanding. p. 160. — same source
- **[RECOLLECTION] Who wrote the exam.** Degenkolb: SEAOC recommended questions and people to write the state examination although the Board officially did it; Gordon Dean served for years on the SEAOC Structural Examination committee, which had engineers write questions and others work the answers before review in committee. p. 162. — same source
- **[RECOLLECTION] Board deference on exam content, 1970s.** Johnston (Board member 1970-78, chair for two years) says the SE testing program was worked out through SEAOC with the profession's backing "right from the start", and that after public members became a 6-5 majority they left exam content and testing standards to the engineer members. pp. 43-46. — [EERI Oral History, Johnston (2012)](https://www.eeri.org/images/oralhistory/20_Johnston.pdf)
- **[RECOLLECTION] References as a screen.** Degenkolb says he refused references to some of his own staff for the structural exam and they were licensed anyway; he saw the exam as testing mathematical ability more than judgment. p. 159. — [EERI Oral History, Degenkolb (1994)](https://www.eeri.org/connections-eeri-oral-history-series)
- **[SECONDARY] End of the fully state-written exam.** NCEES task-force slides by Gregg Brandow define qualifying exams as "16-hour state written structural exams taken before 2004", or NCEES Structural II plus an 8-hour state exam, and describe the later "California SE III" as 8 hours, multiple choice and essay. — [Brandow, NCEES Structural Exam Task Force slides (SELC)](https://www.selicensure.org/sites/default/files/pdf/research/8-GEB-NCEES.pdf)
- **[SNIPPET of a PRIMARY-adjacent source]** A California Regulatory Law Reporter article on the Board (c. 2001-02) reports, per search summary, that the state-specific 16-hour SE exam was costly to develop and administer and caused reciprocity problems, and that the Board planned to replace it with NCEES Structural II (8 hours) plus an 8-hour California exam effective October 2002. The PDF returned 403, so the issue and exact wording are unverified; note the October 2002 target differs from Brandow's "before 2004". — [CRLR, Board for Professional Engineers and Land Surveyors (article 1030 or 1141)](https://digital.sandiego.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1030&context=crlr)
- **[SNIPPET, RECOLLECTION]** A forum poster says that under the 16-hour exam a candidate passed or failed as a whole, and that after the split (which the poster dates to "2003 or 2004" and asks readers to confirm) the two halves could be passed separately. — [Eng-Tips thread 124338](https://www.eng-tips.com/threads/ca-pe-which-exams-needed-if-already-pe.124338/)

### Inferences
- A move from norm-referenced (curve) to criterion-referenced scoring in about 1984-85 would by itself explain a one-off collapse in the number licensed, independent of candidate quality or exam content. That matches the 26-license February 1985 batch, but the link rests on one recollection.
- The October 2002 planned date (CRLR snippet) versus "before 2004" (Brandow) suggests the changeover slipped by at least a year; the brief's "last fully state-written 16-hour exam in 2003" is consistent with Brandow.

### Gaps
- No Board document (minutes, regulation, bulletin) confirming the date of the end of curve grading, the standard-setting method adopted, or the cut score.
- No pass rates for the administrations immediately before and after the change (1983, 1984, 1985, 1986).
- Whether pass rates recovered after the first criterion-referenced exam.
- When the exam went from Degenkolb's open-book 16 hours to any other format, and whether it was ever closed book.

## What pass rates did the Board report in its sunset review reports to the Legislature (c. 1996-97, 2000-01, 2002-03) and annual reports for the SE exam?

### Takeaway
Nothing was retrieved. The 1996-97, 2000-01 and 2002-03 sunset reports and Joint Legislative Sunset Review Committee background papers did not surface in any search; only the 2015 and 2023-24 sunset documents are online, and they cover the NCEES-era exam.

### Cited Findings
- The only structural pass-rate tables found in sunset material are for fiscal years 2018/19 to 2022/23 (overall pass roughly 31-39%), outside the period. — [Assembly B&P Committee, 2024 sunset background paper for BPELSG](https://abp.assembly.ca.gov/system/files/2024-03/board-for-professional-engineers-land-surveyors-and-geologists-sunset-background-paper-2024_1.pdf) (figures seen in search summary only)
- The Board's own published history gives no exam statistics; for the 1930s it says only that the Structural Engineer license was added by the Legislature. — [BPELSG, "History of the Board"](https://www.bpelsg.ca.gov/about_us/history.pdf)
- For comparison, the kind of exam statistic the 1990s sunset process generated: per search summary of a CRLR article, the state land surveyor exam pass rate was 15% (1993), 8-9% (1995), 1.9% (1998), 14% (1999). Land surveyor exam, not SE. — [CRLR, Board for Professional Engineers and Land Surveyors](https://digital.sandiego.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1030&context=crlr)

### Inferences
- Since the CRLR coverage of the sunset reviews quoted multi-year pass rates for the land surveyor exam, the underlying sunset reports very probably contain the same table for the SE exam. That makes the 1996, 2000 and 2002 sunset reports the single best target for a manual retrieval.

### Gaps
- All SE pass rates in the 1996-97, 2000-01 and 2002-03 sunset reports and in Board annual reports. Not found online; likely held by the California State Archives, the California State Library, or obtainable from the Board by records request.

## What do the California Regulatory Law Reporter issues (1980s-1990s) say about SE exam pass rates?

### Takeaway
Could not be answered: every CRLR PDF attempted (digital.sandiego.edu and the core.ac.uk mirror) returned HTTP 403. Search summaries show CRLR discussing the SE exam's cost, reciprocity problems and planned replacement around 2001-02, but no SE pass rate surfaced.

### Cited Findings
- **[SNIPPET]** CRLR describes structural engineering as a title authority tied to the civil practice act that requires an additional examination after qualifying as a civil engineer. — [CRLR article 1110](https://digital.sandiego.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1110&context=crlr)
- **[SNIPPET]** CRLR reports the 16-hour state SE exam was costly and created reciprocity problems, with replacement by Structural II plus an 8-hour state exam planned for October 2002. — [CRLR article 1030](https://digital.sandiego.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1030&context=crlr)
- **[SNIPPET]** A 1985-86 CRLR item notes SB 128 (Montoya), signed 1985, adding the special civil seismic and surveying exam to the 8-hour NCEE civil exam. Civil exam, relevant only as mid-1980s context. — [CRLR issue via CORE mirror](https://core.ac.uk/download/270051797.pdf)

### Inferences
- None beyond the above; the content of the 1980s issues is unknown.

### Gaps
- Whether the 1984-1987 CRLR issues (Vols. 4-7) report the end of curve grading or the low 1984-85 SE results. These are the highest-value issues to open by hand.
- Whether any CRLR issue prints SE candidates/passers by administration.

## Are there figures in SEAOC/SEAONC/SEAOSC publications, EERI oral histories, Board newsletters/bulletins, or STRUCTURE magazine?

### Takeaway
EERI oral histories yield the only usable material (Degenkolb, Johnston, Rinne), all qualitative. Nothing was found in SEAOC/SEAONC/SEAOSC material, STRUCTURE magazine, or the Board bulletins that are online (the online bulletins start well after 2003).

### Cited Findings
- Degenkolb (EERI 1994): curve grading ended; 30+% to 10%; 16-hour open-book exam in his day; SEAOC committee wrote questions. See sections above. — [EERI Oral History Series](https://www.eeri.org/connections-eeri-oral-history-series)
- Johnston (EERI 2012): about 90% first-time pass among his firm's staff; Board member 1970-78. — [EERI, Johnston](https://www.eeri.org/images/oralhistory/20_Johnston.pdf)
- Pregnoff / Rinne (EERI): Rinne's licensing account, no figures; a full-text search of the volume found no pass-rate statement by Pregnoff. — [EERI, Pregnoff and Rinne](https://eeri.org/images/oralhistory/pregnoff.pdf)
- Forell (EERI 2000): a full-text search of the local copy found no exam pass-rate statement. — [EERI Oral History Series](https://www.eeri.org/connections-eeri-oral-history-series)
- STRUCTURE magazine, "Licensure of Structural Engineers" (Edward Major II, Structural Forum): mentions California only as a partial-practice-restriction state; no exam history or pass rates. — [STRUCTURE magazine](https://www.structuremag.org/article/licensure-of-structural-engineers/)
- Board bulletins online (nos. 50-79) report only NCEES-era results, e.g. 2017 SE Lateral 261 tested / 78 passed (30%), Vertical 34%; 2024 Lateral 64% then 20%, Vertical 43% then 28% by half-year. Out of period; useful only as a later benchmark. Seen in search summaries. — [BPELSG Bulletin 72](https://www.bpelsg.ca.gov/pubs/bulletin72.pdf); [BPELSG Bulletin 74](https://www.bpelsg.ca.gov/pubs/bulletin74.pdf)

### Inferences
- The oral histories consistently present the state exam as hard and practitioner-written; none of the interviewees quotes a statewide statistic from a document, which suggests results were circulated in Board newsletters and SEAOC bulletins of the day and not retained in memory as numbers.

### Gaps
- SEAOC convention proceedings and SEAONC/SEAOSC newsletters before 2004: nothing indexed online was found.
- Board newsletters/bulletins from before the current online series: not found online.
- Newspaper or trade-press printings of exam results: not searched in depth (no open archive surfaced).
- The Meehan, Brandow-family and other EERI volumes were not opened in this pass.
- The pre-release Wyllie oral history in `research/sources/` was not read, per instructions.
