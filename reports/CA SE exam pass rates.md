# Exam redesigns repeatedly throttled California SE licensing

California candidates have passed each structural exam component at roughly **30-50% per sitting** for the twenty years that records survive, and the two sharpest drops in California SE licences issued (26 in February 1985; 71 in 2024 and 62 in 2025) both follow a change in how the exam was scored or delivered, not a visible change in the candidates. The record is uneven. For the state-written exam (1931-2003) only **one administration has a documented result** (October 2003: 290 tested, 73 passed, 25.17%); everything earlier is recollection, the most relevant being Henry Degenkolb's mid-1980s remark that the share passing fell from "30+%" to 10% when the Board stopped grading on a curve. From October 2004 the Board published California tested/passed counts for every administration but one (Fall 2017), and NCEES published national first-time and repeat rates, so 2004-2026 is nearly complete. The California-written SE III exam passed 27-36% a year (50% once); the NCEES 16-hour exam passed California candidates at 21-55% per component; and the computer-based exam's buildings depth sections passed **12-33% of California candidates and 13-21% nationally** through 2025 before jumping to **54-63% nationally in April 2026**. The 2024-2025 licence drop is well supported by these numbers: California produced only about 11-18 depth-section passers per sitting. The 1985 link is plausible and arithmetically consistent, but rests on one recollection. No source anywhere reports how many candidates completed the whole exam, so no table below is a "became an SE" rate.

**How to read the evidence flags.** **P** = primary: printed in a Board (BPELS/BPELSG), legislative or NCEES document that a researcher opened. **S** = secondary: a third-party compilation, a recollection, or a figure seen only in a search-engine snippet. **D** = derived or inferred: arithmetic on primary figures, or a reading the source does not itself state. All PDF tables were read through text extraction, and the Board's 2024-2025 section charts were read from greyscale images, so figures flagged "confirm by eye" should be checked against the saved PDFs before publication. "FT" = first-time takers, "Rpt" = repeat takers. California figures are always all takers; the Board never split first-time from repeat, or buildings from bridges.

## The state-written exam left one hard number: 25% in October 2003

No administration-by-administration table of candidates, passers or pass rate was recovered for any California-written SE exam from 1931 through 2002. Every source likely to hold one (California Regulatory Law Reporter issues, the 1996-2003 sunset reports, the Board's old statistics pages for 1999-2002) either refused automated fetching, is not online, or was not transcribed. Every year from 1931 to 2002 should therefore be recorded as a gap. The single exception surfaced from the Board's archived statistics index: the **October 2003** entry is one "Structural" exam, **290 tested, 73 passed, 25.17%**, cut score 650 of 1,500, which is the last fully state-written 16-hour exam (P for the figures; D for the identification as the last state exam) ([archived BPELSG exam statistics index](https://web.archive.org/web/20250222120857/https://www.bpelsg.ca.gov/applicants/exam_statistics.shtml)). That index is described as covering every administration from 1999, so the 1999-2002 SE rows very probably sit on the same page and simply were not transcribed; that is the cheapest gap to close.

The recollections fall into two bands, roughly 25-30+% and roughly 7-20%, and none can be pinned to a year. Roy Johnston's remark that about 90% of his firm's younger staff passed first time describes one Los Angeles firm, not the state, and is excluded from the table ([EERI, Johnston](https://www.eeri.org/images/oralhistory/20_Johnston.pdf)).

The qualitative record does explain the exam's character. Degenkolb recalled a 16-hour open-book structural exam taken after a separate 16-hour civil exam, written in practice by a SEAOC committee although the Board officially set it, and by the mid-1980s taken by hundreds of candidates ([EERI series](https://www.eeri.org/connections-eeri-oral-history-series)). Johnston, a Board member from 1970 to 1978, said the public-member majority left exam content and standards to the engineer members ([EERI, Johnston](https://www.eeri.org/images/oralhistory/20_Johnston.pdf)). Degenkolb is also the only source for the scoring change: the Board was moving from grading on a bell curve, where a set share of each sitting passes, to a fixed minimum standard, and he believed the first exam graded the new way cut the share passing from 30+% to 10%. No Board document confirming the date, the method or the cut score was retrieved, and he does not say which exam he means.

**The February 1985 batch of 26.** The dashboard's licence data show 26 licences in the February 1985 batch against 93 in February 1984 and 148 in March 1986. Degenkolb was interviewed between January 1984 and May 1986 and described the change as just made or under way, which fits a 1984 exam whose passers were licensed in February 1985. The arithmetic also fits: 26 is 28% of the prior year's 93, and 10% is about a third of "30+%" (D, my arithmetic). A switch from norm-referenced to criterion-referenced scoring would by itself produce a one-off collapse of that size without any change in candidates or content, and the rebound to 148 a year later suggests the standard or the candidates' preparation adjusted quickly. This remains an inference resting on one practitioner's impression; the project's existing source index also records that NCEES moved its own exams to criterion-referenced scoring in 1985 ([NCEES history](https://ncees.org/wp-content/uploads/2020_history_web_revised.pdf)), which makes the timing credible but does not say which California exam was first graded that way. The 1984-1987 California Regulatory Law Reporter issues are the best place to confirm it.

The end of the state exam is better documented. Brandow's NCEES task-force slides define the qualifying exams as 16-hour state-written exams taken before 2004, or NCEES Structural II plus an 8-hour state exam afterwards ([Brandow slides](https://www.selicensure.org/sites/default/files/pdf/research/8-GEB-NCEES.pdf)). The Board's pages agree: October 2003 shows one "Structural" exam and October 2004 shows two.

## California's own SE III passed about a third while Structural II passed half

From October 2004 a California candidate needed two exams: the NCEES Structural II (the Board's "National Structural") and the 8-hour California exam (the Board's "State-Specific SE", commonly SE III). The mapping of the Board's labels to those names is an inference from the era; the pages never use either name. The Board published tested, passed and percentage for all California examinees at each sitting, and for the state exam the cut score. NCEES printed national first-time and repeat rates in *Licensure Exchange* without examinee counts.

Three things stand out. First, the **state exam was the tighter gate**: 83-177 California passers a year, 27-36% in every year except October 2009 (50.43%), against 33-51% on the national exam in October sittings. Second, April sittings of the national exam were small (67-91 California takers) and weak (23-38%), consistent with a repeat-heavy pool, though the Board printed no split. Third, the California rate on the national exam (about 49-51% in four of seven October sittings) sits between the national first-time (51-65%) and repeat (21-37%) rates for Structural II, as an all-takers figure should. The April 2011 California row (24.18%) is close to NCEES's national 27% for candidates who sat and passed both components, which suggests the Board's unsplit 2011 rows count whole-exam results (D); either way the 2011 national rows are not comparable with the rows on either side.

## The 16-hour exam held near 44% vertical and 35% lateral for twelve years

NCEES replaced Structural I and II with the 16-hour exam on 8-9 April 2011, set its passing standard with a panel of about 45 structural engineers in June 2011 as a baseline for several years, and required acceptable results on both the Vertical and Lateral components ([LE Aug 2011](https://ncees.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/01/Licensure-Exchange_Licensure-Exchange-August-2011.pdf)). California kept SE III for one last sitting in October 2011 and then relied on the national exam alone; the Legislature later removed the state-exam requirement from statute.

## Depth sections passed 12-20% until April 2026, then 54-63%

The computer-based PE Structural exam began in April 2024. It splits each component into a breadth section (multiple choice, offered year-round) and a depth section (scenario-based alternative item types, April and October only); a candidate must pass all four sections, where the paper exam let a strong multiple-choice half offset a weak essay half within a component ([STRUCTURE, Sept 2024](https://www.structuremag.org/article/what-in-the-world-is-going-on-with-the-new-computer-based-structural-exam/)). California accepts any mix of 16-hour and computer-based component passes, so a candidate holding one paper component in 2023 needed only two new sections.

Against the last paper years the contrast is stark. Paper buildings components passed 26-39% of first-time takers nationally in FY2022-23 and October 2023; the computer-based buildings depth sections passed 13-20% through April 2025, and a candidate had to clear two of them. Breadth held at 40-49% first-time throughout, so the problem was specific to the depth format. First-time buildings volume also roughly halved, from 654 vertical and 529 lateral in FY2022-23 to 342 and 241 depth sittings in FY2024-25.

The profession's complaint was about delivery, not the standard. Candidates reported a single screen, one reference open at a time, slow PDF references, too little time, and no partial credit on the new item types ([STRUCTURE, Sept 2024](https://www.structuremag.org/article/what-in-the-world-is-going-on-with-the-new-computer-based-structural-exam/)); SEAOC and NCSEA told the California Board in April 2025 that they would accept any pass rate provided the exam measured competence and not the test environment ([BPELSG minutes, 3 April 2025](https://www.bpelsg.ca.gov/about_us/meetings/minutes/20250403_bdmn.pdf)). The Board's Executive Officer said in August 2024 that pass rates below 20% were a cause for concern, and SEAOC's representative put the healthy range at 30-50% ([BPELSG minutes, 22 August 2024](https://www.bpelsg.ca.gov/about_us/meetings/minutes/20240822_bdmn.pdf)). NCEES then found the depth sections "speeded" for strong and weak candidates alike, announced an extra 60 minutes from spring 2026 ([WA BRPELS, 28 April 2025](https://brpels.wa.gov/news/2025/structural-exam-changes)), declined a Washington motion, seconded by California and passed by two zones, to shorten the October 2025 exam instead ([BPELSG materials 2025-07-24](https://www.bpelsg.ca.gov/about_us/meetings/materials/20250724_meeting_materials.pdf)), and approved new depth specifications for April 2027 with 48 questions and a new standard setting ([NCEES 2025 Engineering Forum](https://ncees.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/2025-Engineering-Forum-Presentation_FINAL.pdf)). Its own audit committee later reported depth items outside its metrics for difficulty, discrimination and response time ([2026 NCEES Action Items](https://ncees.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/2026-NCEES-Action-Items-and-Conference-Reports_web_2.pdf)).

Rates moved sharply in April 2026: buildings depth first-time rates of 63% (lateral) and 54% (vertical) against 20% and 13% the year before, with repeat takers at 73% and 68%. That window had both the extra hour and, according to the audit committee's charge list, a standard-setting study, and NCEES had said rates would stay stable until a new standard was set. A re-set standard is the likelier main driver, but no source states what changed or apportions the effect, so this is an association in time. California's own half-year rates rose with it, to 46% vertical and 45% lateral for January-June 2026.

The 2024-2025 drop is the best-supported link in the whole record. Multiplying the Board's exams administered by its pass rates gives roughly 16, 13, 11 and 16 California Vertical Depth passers at the four sittings from April 2024 to October 2025, and 12, 13 and 18 Lateral Depth passers at the first three (D, rounded percentages). Since a new SE needs both, completions on the computer-based exam alone could not have exceeded about 11-18 per sitting. Nationally, at most 112 people passed Lateral Depth Buildings and 111 passed Vertical Depth Buildings across the first three windows ([NCEES 2025 Engineering Forum](https://ncees.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/2025-Engineering-Forum-Presentation_FINAL.pdf)). The 71 and 62 licences therefore came mostly from candidates finishing with paper-exam credit, comity applicants, and that small pool; October 2023, the last paper sitting, produced only 24 California Vertical passers. The profession drew the same connection at the Board: SEAOC's representative told the July 2025 meeting that about 70 licences a year was low ([BPELSG minutes, 24 July 2025](https://bpelsg.ca.gov/about_us/meetings/minutes/20250724_bdmn.pdf)), and Washington reported that 35 of its 40 SE licences in 2024 went to comity applicants ([WA BRPELS, 16 June 2025](https://brpels.wa.gov/sites/default/files/2025-06/06-16-25%20SE%20Report.pdf)).

Between redesigns the relationship is loose. The fiscal 2020 and 2021 lows of 80 and 69 followed a cancelled sitting and a systems migration, not weak results, and were made up in the next two years. The decline in California component passers from about 110-150 a year (2012-2013) to 58-95 (2021-2023) did not show in issuance at all. The Board publishes no split of SE licences by exam versus comity, which is the missing piece for any tighter reconciliation.

## Where the sources disagree

**Fall 2015 Lateral, California.** The archived index gives 98 tested and 31 passed (32%); Bulletin 50 prints 31 acceptable, 37 unacceptable, total 68, 46%. The 2018 sunset report's FY 2015/16 rate of 42% matches 98, not 68, so the Bulletin's 37 is probably a misprint for 67. This report uses 98.

**April 2012 Vertical, California.** The standalone page prints 62 passed and 115 failed (summing to 177); the later single-page index prints 63. This report uses 62.

**2024 component labels.** Bulletin 77 gives 81 / 52 / 64% to Lateral and 44 / 19 / 43% to Vertical; the August 2024 and February 2025 Board packets give them the other way round, and the section charts (Vertical Breadth 84 at 64%) support the packets. This report uses the packet labels. One research note quoted the Bulletin labels and its derived "170 lateral sittings, 70 passes" inherits the swap.

**Board fiscal-year roll-ups, FY 2020/21 - 2022/23.** The sunset table's candidate counts (190/163, 210/217, 225/212) do not equal the sums of the per-administration counts (197/166, 241/247, 273/273). Earlier years reconcile exactly. The per-administration figures are preferred.

**"2019" in Bulletin 62.** The California figures are April 2019 alone while the national figures beside them (1,213 and 1,187 tested) cover two administrations. Bulletins 72 and 75 head a California table "NATIONAL".

**WSG against *Squared* 2017.** Godfrey lists vertical repeat rates of 23% (October 2016) and 25% (April 2017); NCEES prints 32% for the fiscal year on 373 repeat takers. One of Godfrey's two values is wrong and no second compilation was found to say which.

**NCEES snapshots are not additive.** *Squared* 2024 plus 2025 first-time depth volumes (439 lateral, 623 vertical buildings) fall slightly short of the Forum's cumulative 447 and 634.

**When the extra hour started.** The Board's May 2025 minutes say October 2025; the NCEES memo, SEAOI and NCSEA say spring 2026. California's Vertical Depth rate did rise from 12% to 29% in October 2025 (national 13% to 21%), before the extra hour on NCEES's dating, so the improvement began one window earlier than the documented change.

**When the new passing standard took effect.** NCEES's forum slides tie the new standard setting to April 2027; its audit committee's charge list names a standard-setting study on the April 2026 exams.

**When the state exam ended.** A California Regulatory Law Reporter item, seen only as a snippet, reports a plan to switch in October 2002; Brandow says before 2004, and the Board's October 2003 and October 2004 pages support Brandow.

**Structural II in 2004.** A forum recollection of about 16% first-time "around 2004-2005" contradicts the 56% NCEES printed for October 2004. Structural II also shows an identical 65 / 36 in April and October 2009, read from separate issues and worth a second look.

**First-time versus repeat tables on the old Board site.** One note hoped the retired `multistats.shtml` page held SE splits; the archived copy has no structural content ([archived multistats page](https://web.archive.org/web/20151110011348/http://www.bpelsg.ca.gov/applicants/multistats.shtml)).

## Conclusion

The pass rate that matters for licence counts is one nobody publishes: the share of candidates who finish the whole exam. Component and section rates in the 30-50% range coexisted for two decades with 117-169 licences a year, because repeat sittings, banked components and comity smoothed the flow. What breaks the flow is a discontinuity that strands the candidates in the pipeline, which is what a re-based passing standard did, on the available evidence, in 1984-85 and what four separately passed sections with a 13-20% depth rate did in 2024-25. For the dashboard, that argues for plotting exam changes as events and treating the per-sitting rates as context, with the California all-takers series (2004-2026) as the primary line and the national first-time series as a comparison, kept visually separate because they measure different populations.

Two expectations follow. If the April 2026 rates hold and the backlog of repeat takers clears (161 and 152 repeat sittings in the two buildings depth sections that window), California issuance should rebound in late 2026 and 2027 much as it did in fiscal 2022; the April 2027 respecification is the next point where it could stall again. And the pre-2004 record is recoverable: the 1999-2003 Board pages, the 1996-2003 sunset reports and the 1984-87 Regulatory Law Reporter issues together would turn the 1985 story from an inference into a documented event.
